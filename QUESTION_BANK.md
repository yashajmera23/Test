# Question Bank

**What a skill asks when it needs to know something it can't work out.**

The system drafts first — that's rule 1. But a draft still has holes, and this is where the questions come from when there's genuinely nothing to draft from.

Structure is Jesse James Garrett's five planes from *The Elements of User Experience*, because a gap is almost always a gap on a specific plane, and naming the plane tells you which question to ask. The principle triggers at the end are from *Universal Principles of Design*.

**How to use it:** never dump the bank. Find the plane where the gap sits, ask **two to four** questions from it, **one at a time**, and wait.

**Always show something before you ask.** A question against a blank space is not rigour, it's a beginner staring at a cursor. Draft what you can, then ask about the part you couldn't.

---

## Plane 1 — Strategy
*Why does this exist, and for whom?* Gaps here are the expensive ones — everything above inherits them.

- What does the person get out of this that they can't get today?
- What do people do instead, right now? Every problem has an incumbent, even if it's a WhatsApp group or a notebook.
- Which single number moves if this works? Name it, and say who watches it.
- Who is this explicitly **not** for? If it's for everyone, it's grounded in nothing.
- Describe the last real person you spoke to who has this problem. What were they doing when it bit them?
- If this succeeded completely, what would that person stop doing?
- Which of your research findings would have to be wrong for this to be a bad idea?

## Plane 2 — Scope
*What's in, and — harder — what's out?*

- Name three things a reasonable person would expect this to do that it won't.
- Which single capability, if removed, makes the whole thing pointless?
- Which capability is here because it's genuinely required, versus because it felt incomplete without it?
- What are you deliberately doing badly in order to do one thing well?
- What data does this need that you don't have and can't get?
- What happens on day one, before there's any content, history, or other users?
- Is this one product or two? Say it in one sentence with no "and" — if you can't, it's two.

## Plane 3 — Structure
*How is it organised, and what happens in what order?*

- Walk me through the single most important path, place by place, from the moment they open it.
- Where does a first-time person land, and where does a returning one land? If the same, why?
- What is the *one* thing this place is for? If two, which loses when they compete?
- What can go wrong on this path, and what does the person see when it does?
- What has to be true before this path can start — an account, a connection, data, permission?
- Where does the person leave, satisfied? Name the exit.
- **Feature addition:** which existing screen does this hang off, and what was on that screen before?

## Plane 4 — Skeleton
*Where does everything sit, and how is it found?*

- On the main place, what's the single most important element, and how does a person know that within one second?
- How do they move between the main areas — tabs, stack, drawer, something else? Why that one?
- What's on screen when there's nothing yet — no items, no history, no results?
- What do they see while waiting, and how long is the wait honestly likely to be?
- Which action is destructive or hard to undo, and what stands between the person and doing it accidentally?
- How does someone get back? Every place needs an answer.
- What's the smallest screen this has to work on, and what falls off it?

## Plane 5 — Surface
*What does it look and sound like?*

- Show me two or three products whose **interaction model** — not their looks — is close to what you're building. What specifically is close?
- Is this dense and functional, or spacious and calm? Pick one. It changes more than the palette does.
- Read me two real strings from your interface. Formal or casual? Terse or explanatory?
- Which single colour is the "tap this" colour, and is it used for anything else? It shouldn't be.
- What tone should someone feel when an error appears — apologetic, neutral, matter-of-fact?

## Principle triggers

Use these when the plane questions have been answered and something is still structurally wrong. Each is a question, not a lecture — the point is that the student sees the flaw, not that you name the principle.

**Hick's Law** — how many choices does the person face at the moment of decision, and can any be deferred or removed?

**Fitts's Law** — the action you most want tapped: how big is it, and how far from where the thumb already rests?

**Progressive disclosure** — what's on screen right now that they don't need until later? Why is it early?

**Recognition over recall** — what does this expect them to remember from a previous place? Can you show it instead?

**Feedback** — after every action here, how does the person know it worked? Name the signal for each.

**Constraint** — what stops them entering something that can't be valid? Prevention beats an error message.

**Forgiveness** — what's the most expensive mistake available here, and what's the undo?

**Mapping** — does the arrangement of controls match the arrangement of what they affect, or do they have to translate?

**Signal-to-noise** — remove everything not carrying information. What's left? Is anything important now missing?

**Chunking** — they have to hold something in their head across steps. How many pieces? Under five?

**Von Restorff** — if everything is emphasised, nothing is. What's the *one* emphasised thing here?

**Affordance** — for each interactive element, what tells them it's interactive, apart from convention?

**Hierarchy** — squint at it. What do you see first, second, third? Is that the order you intended?

**Aesthetic-usability effect** — people rate attractive things as more usable, including in your own usability test. What did you observe them *do*, not say?

## Rules for asking

**One at a time.** A block of eight questions gets one block of eight thin answers. A single question gets a real one, and the answer usually reshapes the next question.

**Follow the vague answer, don't accept it.** "Young professionals" isn't a user. "Easy to use" isn't a success signal. Ask again, narrower — and offer two concrete options if they're stuck.

**Say why you're asking.** *"I'm asking because I've got nothing to put in the metric field, and if I guess it, everything you build in week two gets measured against a number I made up."* People answer better questions when they can see the hole.

**When they're stuck, give more — not less.** A second example. A narrower question. Three options to react to. Never "think about it and come back".

**Stop when the gap is closed.** This is a resource, not a script. Four good answers beat twelve.
