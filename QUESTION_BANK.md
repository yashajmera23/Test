# Question Bank

**This file is the alternative to making things up.**

When a skill hits a gap it cannot fill from what the student supplied, it does not invent. It comes here, finds the questions that would close that gap, and hands them back.

The structure is Jesse James Garrett's five planes from *The Elements of User Experience* — because a gap is almost always a gap on a specific plane, and naming the plane tells you which question to ask. The principle triggers in the second half are drawn from *Universal Principles of Design* (Lidwell, Holden & Butler).

**How to use it:** never dump the whole bank. Find the plane where the gap sits, ask **two to four** questions from it, one at a time, and wait.

---

## Plane 1 — Strategy
*Why does this exist, and for whom?*
Gaps here are the expensive ones. Everything above inherits them.

- What does the person get out of this that they cannot get today?
- What do people do instead, right now? Every problem has an incumbent, even if it is a WhatsApp group or a notebook.
- Which single number moves if this works? Name it, and say who watches it.
- Who is this explicitly **not** for? If it is for everyone, it is grounded in nothing.
- Describe the last real person you spoke to who has this problem. What were they doing when it bit them?
- If this succeeded completely, what would that person stop doing?
- What does the business or organisation get? Even a concept project has an implied model.
- Which of your research findings would have to be wrong for this to be a bad idea?

## Plane 2 — Scope
*What is in, and — harder — what is out?*

- Name three things a reasonable person would expect this to do that it will not do.
- Which single capability, if removed, makes the whole thing pointless?
- Which capability is here because it is genuinely required, versus because it felt incomplete without it?
- What are you deliberately doing badly in order to do one thing well?
- What data does this need that you do not have and cannot get?
- What happens on day one, before there is any content, history, or other users?
- Is this one product or two? Say it in one sentence with no "and" — if you cannot, it is two.

## Plane 3 — Structure
*How is it organised, and what happens in what order?*

- Walk me through the single most important path, screen by screen, from the moment the person opens it.
- Where does a first-time user land, and where does a returning user land? If the same, why?
- What is the *one* thing this screen is for? If two, which loses when they compete?
- What can go wrong on this path, and what does the person see when it does?
- What has to be true before this path can start — an account, a connection, data, permission?
- Where does the person leave, satisfied? Name the exit.
- **Feature addition only:** which existing screen does this hang off, and what was on that screen before?

## Plane 4 — Skeleton
*Where does everything sit, and how is it found?*

- On the main screen, what is the single most important element, and how does a person know that within one second?
- How does the person move between the main areas — tabs, stack, drawer, something else? Why that one?
- What is on screen when there is nothing yet — no items, no history, no results?
- What does the person see while waiting, and how long is the wait honestly likely to be?
- Which action is destructive or hard to undo, and what stands between the person and doing it accidentally?
- How does someone get back? Every screen needs an answer.
- What is the smallest screen this has to work on, and what falls off it?

## Plane 5 — Surface
*What does it look and sound like?*

- Show me two or three products whose **interaction model** — not their looks — is close to what you are building. What specifically is close?
- Is this dense and functional, or spacious and calm? Pick one. It changes more than the palette does.
- Read me two real strings from your interface. Formal or casual? Terse or explanatory?
- Which single colour is the "tap this" colour, and is it used for anything else? It should not be.
- What tone should someone feel when an error appears — apologetic, neutral, matter-of-fact?

## Principle triggers

*Universal Principles of Design.* Use these when the student has answered the plane questions but something is still structurally wrong. Each is a question, not a lecture — the point is that the student sees the flaw, not that you name the principle.

**Hick's Law** — how many choices does the person face at the moment of decision, and can any be deferred or removed?

**Fitts's Law** — the action you most want tapped: how big is it, and how far is it from where the thumb already rests?

**Progressive disclosure** — what is on screen right now that the person does not need until later? Why is it early?

**Recognition over recall** — what does this expect the person to remember from a previous screen? Can you show it instead?

**Feedback** — after every action here, how does the person know it worked? Name the signal for each one.

**Constraint** — what stops the person entering something that cannot be valid? Prevention beats an error message.

**Forgiveness** — what is the most expensive mistake available here, and what is the undo?

**Mapping** — does the arrangement of controls match the arrangement of what they affect, or does the person have to translate?

**Signal-to-noise** — remove everything that is not carrying information. What is left? Is anything important now missing?

**Chunking** — the person has to hold something in their head across steps. How many pieces? Under five?

**Von Restorff** — if everything is emphasised, nothing is. What is the *one* emphasised thing on this screen?

**Affordance** — for each interactive element, what tells the person it is interactive, apart from convention?

**Hierarchy** — squint at the screen. What do you see first, second, third? Is that the order you intended?

**Aesthetic-usability effect** — people rate attractive things as more usable, including in your usability test. What did you observe them *do*, not say?

## Rules for asking

**One at a time.** A block of eight questions gets one block of eight thin answers. A single question gets a real one, and the answer usually reshapes the next question.

**Follow the vague answer, do not accept it.** "Young professionals" is not a user. "Easy to use" is not a success signal. Ask again, narrower.

**Say why you are asking.** *"I'm asking because I have nothing to put in the success-signal field, and if I guess it, everything you build in week two gets measured against a number I made up."* Students answer better questions when they can see the hole.

**Stop when the gap is closed.** The bank is a resource, not a script. Four good answers beat twelve.

**If they say `/molecule-anyway`, stop asking.** Record what was never answered, stamp the output, move on.
